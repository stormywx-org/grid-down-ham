What it is

Grid Down Ham is a Stormy Weather Response Force (SWRF) resource. When phones and the internet are down, a handheld radio can still reach people. This page builds a one-family sheet for your address: the crash-course steps, a Mayday script, NOAA Weather Radio for your county, and a place for local repeater frequencies.

NOAA Weather Radio is filled in when the official NWS coverage file has a match for your county. Amateur repeater rows are left blank for you to write in from RepeaterBook or from what you confirm on the air.

How the lookup works

  1. 1Enter your addressA complete U.S. street address with city and state works best.
  2. 2We geocode itThe U.S. Census Bureau Geocoder finds the coordinates and county. If the match differs from what you typed, we say so.
  3. 3We fill what can be verifiedNOAA Weather Radio comes from the official NWS county coverage file. Amateur repeater rows are left blank for you to write in.
  4. 4You print the sheetRead it on this page, then print or save it as a PDF to keep with the radio.

If the grid is down — nine steps

These are the same crash-course steps printed on page 1 of every Grid Down Radio Guide.

  1. 1Antenna on tight first, then power on.Transmitting without an antenna can damage the radio. Seat the antenna firmly before you turn the radio on.
  2. 2Switch to frequency mode.Use the VFO or V/M button (the label varies by radio) so you can type or tune a frequency, not only saved channels.
  3. 3Set squelch, then listen first.Turn squelch down until you hear static, then back up just until it is quiet. Listen or scan before you transmit.
  4. 4Start on the national calling channels.146.520 MHz (2 meter) and 446.000 MHz (70 centimeter) are the ARRL national FM simplex calling frequencies. Simplex is radio-to-radio on the same frequency and works without a repeater.
  5. 5Press, pause, talk, release.Press the transmit button, pause one second, talk slowly 2 to 3 inches from the microphone, then release to listen.
  6. 6Make the call.For a life-threatening emergency, say “Mayday” three times. For other urgent help, say “Emergency traffic.” Give your name, location, what happened, how many people, and what you need. Repeat the message, then listen a full minute.
  7. 7If no one answers, improve your position and retry.Move to higher ground, away from buildings, and keep the antenna vertical. Try again on a schedule, such as at the top of each hour, and save battery.
  8. 8Local repeaters reach farther.Use the frequencies on your local sheet. A repeater needs the listed offset and tone; without them, your call may not go through.
  9. 9Other radios you may already have.FRS and GMRS: any channel may be used for emergency communications (FCC); some groups pre-arrange channel 1 or 20 — confirm locally. CB channel 9 is reserved for emergency communications or traveler assistance. NOAA Weather Radio is receive-only.

Mayday / emergency script

Make the call

  1. For a life-threatening emergency, say “Mayday” three times. For other urgent help, say “Emergency traffic.”
  2. Give your name, and your call sign if you have one.
  3. Give your location — address, crossroads, landmarks, or GPS.
  4. Say what happened.
  5. Say how many people are with you, and whether anyone is injured.
  6. Say what you need.
  7. Repeat the message, then listen a full minute before trying again.

Press the transmit button, pause one second, talk 2 to 3 inches from the microphone, then release to listen.

Key simplex and emergency frequencies

146.520

2-meter national FM simplex calling

ARRL national calling frequency. Radio-to-radio, no repeater required. Listen first.

446.000

70-centimeter national FM simplex calling

ARRL national calling frequency on the 70 cm band. Listen first, then make a short, clear call.

FRS / GMRS

Any channel may be used in an emergency

The FCC allows emergency communications on FRS and GMRS. Some groups pre-arrange channel 1 or 20 — confirm locally.

CB 9 · NOAA

CB channel 9 and weather radio

CB channel 9 is reserved for emergency communications or traveler assistance. NOAA Weather Radio (162.400–162.550 MHz) is receive-only.

How to get licensed

An amateur radio license is required to transmit on amateur frequencies. FCC rule 97.403 provides that no Part 97 rule prevents an amateur station from using any means of radiocommunication at its disposal to provide essential communication needs in connection with the immediate safety of human life and immediate protection of property when normal communication systems are not available.

Get a Technician license before you need it. Study at HamStudy.org and find an exam session through the ARRL. The entry-level Technician exam is a multiple-choice written test; there is no Morse-code requirement.

Good to know — verify on the air

Addresses are geocoded with the U.S. Census Bureau Geocoder (Public Address Ranges — Current Benchmark). This tool is for U.S. addresses only.

NOAA Weather Radio stations come from the official National Weather Service county coverage file (CCL.js) that feeds weather.gov/nwr/counties. Reception depends on terrain; confirm you can hear the station.

Amateur repeater listings are not invented. Repeater rows stay blank unless an approved RepeaterBook Export API is later configured. Look up nearby machines on RepeaterBook.com and write them in. When RepeaterBook data is used, attribution is: Data courtesy of RepeaterBook.com. Listings can be out of date — verify on the air.

Local ARES nets, SKYWARN times, and emergency-management contacts cannot be reliably auto-verified for a new address. The generated sheet gives search links and blank rows.

Your address is used only to build the sheet. This draft does not publish lookups. If a logging sheet is added later, it would be a private SWRF spreadsheet, not a public directory.
